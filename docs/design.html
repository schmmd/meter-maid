Android app design · tested against your meter

Point at the drums, confirm, done.

A single-purpose Android app that replaces the photo → squint → retype → spreadsheet chore with one framed shot and one confirmation tap. On-device OCR reads the register; you stay the final authority on every digit — and after measuring it against your own photos, on one digit in particular.

Your Rockwell 3/4 T-04, as photographed 24 Oct 2021 — four dark-on-light drums, two light-on-dark, one painted zero, and the sweep needle straight through the middle

34 23 45 45 45 45 00

The sixth drum — caught mid-roll, light-on-dark, with the needle beside it — is the entire engineering problem, and the measurements below say so out loud.

What the photos showed

15 photos, Nov 2017 – Oct 2021, run through a general-purpose OCR

Rather than guess at how on-device OCR would cope, I ran your photos through Apple's Vision text recognizer — a close analogue of ML Kit's: the same class of general scene-text model, with the same failure modes. The results changed several decisions in this design, and turned up a bug in the voting scheme I had originally written.

Your meter, specified

  • Rockwell International 3/4 T-04, in gallons. Seven positions: six rotating drums plus a painted trailing zero that never moves.
  • Mixed polarity within one register. The leading four drums are dark digits on a light ground; the last two are light on dark. There is no single preprocessing pass that suits both.
  • The red sweep needle crosses the digits at most needle positions, splitting the register visually in two.
  • The dial face is crowded with competing text — "Rockwell International", "Pittsburgh, PA 15208", "GALLONS", "3/4 T-04", and the 0–9 numerals ringing the face.
  • The meter was replaced at least once. Your July 2019 photo is a different unit entirely — a Master Meter 5/8" BL, newly installed, reading 0000129.
  • Orientation is arbitrary. Roughly half your photos have the register running vertically, because of how the meter sits in the crawlspace.

A bug this testing found

My original vote counted agreement only among frames that survived a digit-count filter. On the jitter test, 8 of 10 frames were discarded and the surviving 2 agreed — so it reported unanimous confidence in 324442, whose last digit was wrong. Agreement must be measured against frames attempted, not frames surviving, and a minimum survivor count must be enforced. Both fixes are in the corrected pipeline.

The honest summary: general on-device OCR handles the slow-moving leading digits on your meter well, and cannot read the one drum that is actually in motion. That is a workable result — but only for a design that treats OCR as a prefill for five digits and asks you for the sixth, rather than one that presents seven digits and hopes.

Where this leaves ML Kit

You picked on-device, and this design stays on-device — it is free, offline, private, and instant, which matters in a crawlspace with no signal. But these numbers are why the recognizer sits behind a one-method interface: if the five-digit prefill turns out not to be worth the tap you save, swapping in a cloud vision model is one class and a settings toggle. Milestone 3 in the build order is deliberately the cheapest thing in the plan to throw away.

The loop, end to end

What the app has to feel like before anything else matters

Today the chore is four context switches: phone camera, photo library, mental arithmetic, spreadsheet app. Each one is a place to stop and not come back, which is why the readings aren't regular. The app's only real job is collapsing that into one uninterrupted motion you can finish in a crawlspace.

  1. Open

    The app launches straight into the viewfinder. No home screen, no menu. A quick-settings tile and a home-screen shortcut both land here too.

  2. Frame

    A guide rectangle sits over the register, with a rotation control — because half your photos have it running vertically. You line the drums up inside it; OCR runs on the live preview, so digits appear ghosted under the frame as soon as they resolve.

  3. Shutter

    One tap captures a still and banks the last handful of analysis frames. The photo is kept — it's your audit trail when a number looks wrong three months later, and given what the OCR does to that last drum, you will want it.

  4. Confirm

    Six digit boxes. The first five prefilled and usually correct; the last one empty and focused, with the keyboard already up. Today's date, prefilled. The change since your last reading, computed live as you type.

  5. Save

    Writes to the phone immediately and returns to the viewfinder. The row reaches your spreadsheet whenever the phone next has signal — you do not wait for it, and you do not need to remember it.

The design bet

Every second of friction has to come out of your path, not the app's. It's fine for the app to spend a second chewing on five frames of OCR; it's not fine to make you tap through a save dialog, pick a sheet, or wait on a network round trip under the house.

Reading the drums

The corrected pipeline, after measurement

The measurements rewrote three of the five stages I originally proposed. What survives is simpler than what I started with, because most of the image processing turned out to be actively harmful.

  1. Crop to the guide rectangle, and rotate

    Only pixels inside the guide reach the recognizer. This is the single highest-value step: it removes the serial number, "PA 15208", "GALLONS" and the dial numerals, all of which the OCR otherwise offers as candidate readings. The guide is rotatable and its angle is remembered, because your meter reads vertically in the crawlspace.

  2. Upscale about 2×, and do nothing else

    No greyscale, no contrast stretch, no thresholding, no inversion. Every one of those made the recognizer return nothing on your register, because its two halves have opposite polarity and any threshold that rescues one destroys the other. 5× upscaling was also worse than 2×.

  3. Recognize five frames

    ImageAnalysis is already producing frames; bank the five around the shutter press. Between frames your hand moves a millimetre and the recognizer makes different mistakes, which is what makes them cancel.

  4. Reassemble fragments, then vote per position

    Frames often come back split — 324 and 442 rather than 324442. Sort the pieces by x-position and concatenate rather than discarding them; on the jitter test that recovers roughly half the frames the naive filter threw away. Then vote position by position, with agreement measured against frames attempted.

  5. Prefill only what earned it

    Positions below the agreement threshold are left blank rather than filled with a guess. On your meter that means five digits prefilled and the moving drum left to you — which is honest, and is also one keystroke.

The vote, with both fixes and fragment reassembly:

fun vote(frames: List<List<Fragment>>, expect: Int, minFrames: Int = 3): RecognizedReading? {
    // A frame often arrives split ("324" + "442"). Rebuild it in x order
    // instead of discarding it — that recovered ~half the dropped frames.
    val attempted = frames.size
    val usable = frames
        .map { f -> f.sortedBy { it.boxLeft }.joinToString("") { it.text }.filter(Char::isDigit) }
        .filter { it.length == expect }

    // Too few survivors means the framing failed, not that the digits are known.
    if (usable.size < minFrames) return null

    val digits = StringBuilder()
    val agreement = mutableListOf<Float>()
    for (i in 0 until expect) {
        val counts = usable.groupingBy { it[i] }.eachCount()
        val (digit, n) = counts.maxBy { it.value }
        digits.append(digit)
        // Denominator is every frame we tried, so dropped frames lower confidence
        // rather than silently raising it. This was the bug.
        agreement += n.toFloat() / attempted
    }
    return RecognizedReading(digits.toString(), agreement, "mlkit-v2")
}

A constraint worth exploiting later

The app knows your previous reading, and your meter moves roughly 120 gallons a day. That bounds the next reading tightly — the leading four digits are nearly always predictable. Scoring OCR candidates against that range would catch exactly the failure seen on the Master Meter photo, where the recognizer returned a serial number as the reading. Worth building once the basic loop is proven, not before.

Screens

Three, and a settings page you visit twice

Capture

Full-bleed viewfinder, torch toggle, one shutter, and a guide rectangle you can rotate as well as resize. Set once against your meter's actual orientation, then remembered.

Confirm

Digits are individual boxes, not a text field. The moving drum's box is focused on arrival with the numeric keypad already up, so the common case is: glance, one key, Save.

History

Date, reading, change, gallons per day, and a sync dot. Tap a row to see its original photograph — the reason the photo is archived at all.

Settings

Sheet destination, drum count, whether a trailing painted zero is ignored, the plausible daily-use band, and a "meter replaced" action that resets the monotonic check.

Data model

One local table, one sheet layout that keeps its own formulas

The local record keeps more than the spreadsheet needs, deliberately — the extra fields are what let you answer "why does this number look wrong?" later without guessing.

@Entity(tableName = "readings")
data class Reading(
    @PrimaryKey val id: String,     // UUID; doubles as the sheet's idempotency key
    val readingDate: String,        // "2026-08-31" local date; editable
    val capturedAtUtc: Long,        // epoch millis; never edited
    val value: Long,                // the six drums, painted zero excluded
    val meterId: String,            // "rockwell-3/4-t04" — resets the monotonic check
    val ocrRaw: String?,            // what the recognizer actually returned
    val minAgreement: Float?,       // lowest per-digit agreement, 0..1
    val corrected: Boolean,         // did you change a prefilled digit?
    val photoPath: String,          // app-private storage
    val note: String,
    val sync: SyncState             // QUEUED | SYNCING | SYNCED | FAILED
)

meterId exists because your history already spans a meter change. Without it, the first reading on a new unit trips the monotonic check and looks like a catastrophic misread; with it, the app knows to compare only within a run.

Keeping capturedAtUtc separate from readingDate matters more than it looks. You will occasionally photograph the meter on Sunday and save it on Monday, or fix a date you fat-fingered. The capture instant is forensic; the reading date is editorial.

The sheet

The app writes six columns and nothing else. Everything derived is a formula that lives in the sheet, so it stays correct when you edit a row by hand — and so the app never has to recompute history.

ColHeaderWritten byNotes
AidappUUID. Makes retries safe.
BdateappA real date value, not text.
CreadingappSix drums, gallons.
DmeterappWhich unit produced it.
Esourceappocr or corrected — lets you audit how much the OCR is actually earning.
FnoteappUsually empty.
GchangeformulaGallons since previous row.
HdaysformulaElapsed days.
Igal/dayformulaThe number you actually care about.

Put these three in G3, H3 and I3 once. They spill down forever and need no maintenance as rows arrive:

G3: =ARRAYFORMULA(IF(C3:C="","", IF(D3:D<>D2:D, "new meter", C3:C - C2:C)))
H3: =ARRAYFORMULA(IF(B3:B="","", B3:B - B2:B))
I3: =ARRAYFORMULA(IF(B3:B="","", IF(D3:D<>D2:D, "", (C3:C - C2:C) / (B3:B - B2:B))))

The D3:D<>D2:D guard is what stops a meter swap from rendering as a nonsensical negative change — the same problem meterId solves on the phone.

Sheets sync

Two viable paths, and they are not close in setup cost

Apps Script web app recommended

Twenty lines of JavaScript bound to your spreadsheet, deployed as a web endpoint. The app sends a JSON POST. No OAuth, no consent screen, no Google Cloud project, no app verification.

Sheets REST API v4

Proper OAuth via Credential Manager and AuthorizationClient, a Cloud project, an OAuth client tied to your signing certificate, and a consent screen. Correct, and considerably more work.

For an app with exactly one user writing to exactly one spreadsheet, the Apps Script path is the right call. It moves all the credential handling into a URL you paste into settings, and it lets you change the sheet's shape later without touching the APK.

const SHEET_ID = '1AbC…';
const SECRET   = 'paste-a-long-random-string';

function doPost(e) {
  const body = JSON.parse(e.postData.contents);
  if (body.secret !== SECRET) return json({ ok: false, error: 'bad secret' });

  const sheet = SpreadsheetApp.openById(SHEET_ID).getSheetByName('Readings');
  const ids = sheet.getRange('A:A').getValues().flat().filter(String);

  // The phone retries anything it didn't see succeed — including writes that did.
  if (ids.indexOf(body.id) !== -1) return json({ ok: true, duplicate: true });

  // NOT appendRow(): the spilled formulas in G:I count as content, so appendRow
  // would land below them. Column A is the only honest row counter here.
  const row = ids.length + 1;
  sheet.getRange(row, 1, 1, 6).setValues([[
    body.id,
    new Date(body.readingDate + 'T12:00:00'),  // noon dodges DST edges
    body.value,
    body.meterId,
    body.corrected ? 'corrected' : 'ocr',
    body.note || ''
  ]]);
  return json({ ok: true });
}

function json(obj) {
  return ContentService.createTextOutput(JSON.stringify(obj))
    .setMimeType(ContentService.MimeType.JSON);
}
Know what the secret is

Deployed as "anyone can access", that URL plus the shared string is effectively a bearer token, and anything shipped in an APK can be extracted. For a personal app writing to your own well log this is a fine trade — the worst case is someone appending junk to a spreadsheet you can undo. Keep the URL out of any public repo, and if you ever publish the app, move to the OAuth path.

If you'd rather do it properly from the start, the REST equivalent is a single authenticated call — the work is all in obtaining the token, not in spending it:

POST https://sheets.googleapis.com/v4/spreadsheets/{sheetId}/values/Readings!A:F:append
     ?valueInputOption=USER_ENTERED&insertDataOption=INSERT_ROWS
Authorization: Bearer {token}    // scope: .../auth/spreadsheets

{ "values": [["a3f1…", "2021-10-24", 324444, "rockwell-t04", "corrected", ""]] }

USER_ENTERED is the important flag: it makes Sheets parse 2021-10-24 into a real date the way it would if you had typed it, rather than storing a string that silently breaks column H's arithmetic.

Offline queue

Because a crawlspace is where signal goes to die

The save button never touches the network. It writes to Room, enqueues a worker, and returns you to the viewfinder. Everything after that is the system's problem.

The worker takes every QUEUED row in date order, posts each, and marks results. WorkManager's NetworkType.CONNECTED constraint means it doesn't wake up until there's a connection, and its backoff policy handles the flaky-signal case without any retry code of your own.

Validation rules

Cheap checks that catch what the OCR gets wrong

These run on the confirm screen, live, as you edit. Only the first is a hard stop — the rest warn and let you proceed, because a real meter occasionally does something surprising and the app should never be the reason a true reading goes unlogged.

RuleCheckWhat you see
Digit count All six boxes filled Save disabled until complete. The moving drum is never auto-filled, so this is a real gate, not a formality.
Monotonic value ≥ previous reading on the same meter Red: "meters don't run backwards — check the leading digits." Cleared automatically after a meter swap.
Rate plausible Gallons ÷ elapsed days inside your band Amber with the computed rate spelled out. Your history runs roughly 60–150 gal/day, so the default band is 20–600 and you narrow it later.
Leading digits High-order drums changed more than the rate allows Amber on the specific digit. This is the check that would have caught 1041993 being offered when the register said 0000129.
Duplicate date A row already exists for this date Choice: replace that row, or add anyway.
Stale date Reading date more than 7 days before capture Amber confirm — usually means the date picker was left on an old value.

Permissions

One dangerous permission, and it's the obvious one

  • CAMERA — the only runtime permission the app requests.
  • INTERNET — install-time, for the sheet append.
  • No location. Nothing about the app needs it, and a photo of your wellhead with coordinates attached is not a thing to create casually.
  • No storage permission. Photos live in app-private filesDir. They don't appear in your gallery, and they're removed with the app.

The photographs never leave the phone unless you explicitly enable archiving them to Drive, which is a later, optional feature. The only data crossing the network is six short fields per reading.

Build order

Deliberately OCR-last, and the measurements make that ordering look better, not worse

The riskiest component is the one whose value is now measured rather than assumed: OCR saves you five keystrokes out of six, not six out of six. That is worth having, and it is emphatically not worth blocking the app on. So it goes last, on top of something already useful.

  1. The loop, with typing

    Camera, capture, confirm screen, Room, history list. You type all six digits. Already a real improvement: framed photo, prefilled date, one screen, no app switching. This is also where you find out whether the ergonomics work in the crawlspace.

  2. Backfill from your archive

    An import that reads the dates out of your existing filenames — PXL_20211024_195922840 is already an ISO date — shows you the photo and takes the number. Fifteen photos, a few minutes, and the sheet starts with four years of history instead of one row.

  3. Sheets, end to end

    Apps Script endpoint, WorkManager sync, the state machine above. At this point the chore is genuinely solved — everything after is speed.

  4. OCR

    Rotatable ROI crop, 2× upscale, five-frame vote with fragment reassembly, threshold prefill. Ship it behind a settings toggle so you can turn it off, and so you can measure it honestly against the source column.

  5. Validation and comforts

    The rules table, history-constrained candidate scoring, quick-settings tile, CSV export, optional Drive photo archive.

A useful side effect

Milestone 1 stores each photo alongside a hand-typed, definitely-correct number. Using the app for a few weeks builds a labelled test set of your meter in your lighting — so by milestone 4 you can measure the recognizer's real accuracy on your register instead of extrapolating from the fifteen photos I had.

Open questions

The photos answered most of the original list. These are what's left

  • Is the trailing painted zero part of your recorded number? I've assumed not: reading six drums gives roughly 120 gal/day across your history, which is right for a household, while including the zero gives 1,200. Worth confirming, because it's a factor of ten in every row.
  • What happened with the Master Meter? Your July 2019 photo shows a different, newly installed unit reading 0000129, but the Rockwell continues in your later photos. Is that a second meter, a replacement that was reverted, or a different property? It determines whether meterId needs to be a picker on the capture screen or just a settings value.
  • Your existing spreadsheet's layout. The app should append to the sheet you already have rather than make you migrate. Paste the current header row and the column mapping adjusts to match it.
  • Do you want the readings backfilled now? I can extract the numbers from all fifteen photos and hand you a CSV keyed by the dates in the filenames — that's useful whether or not the app ever gets built.
